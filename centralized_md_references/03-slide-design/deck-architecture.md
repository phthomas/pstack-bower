# Deck Architecture and Storyboarding

How a storyline becomes a sequence of slides. Sources: Zelazny *Say It with Presentations*
(ch. "Craft the Story Line", "Build a Storyboard"); mbb-deck; Dave McKinsey; Stanley & Castles
ch.4 and Appendix B; McKinsey Staff Paper No. 66.

---

## 1. Answer first — Zelazny's demonstration

The clearest argument in the corpus for conclusion-first structure, and it takes 30 seconds.

**The Shirley letter, chronological:**
> Dear Shirley,
> Remember last Saturday afternoon when I was playing in the park with my boyfriend and you
> came over, and he told me that when my back was turned, you kissed him?
> And also, on Sunday when you came to my house and my Mom made you a tuna fish salad for lunch
> and you said: "Yech! That's the worst salad I ever ate!"?
> And yesterday, when my cat brushed against your leg, you kicked her and threatened to sic
> your dog "Monster" on her?
> Well, for all of these reasons, I hate you, and I no longer want to be your friend. — Lucy

**The same letter, bottom-up:**
> Dear Shirley,
> I HATE you. Here are my reasons:
> 1. You stole my boyfriend.
> 2. You insulted my mother.
> 3. You scared my cat.

Zelazny's business version — the same deck, two orders. Nod when you know the recommendation:

**Problem-solving order (the default temptation):**
```
OBJECTIVE     To determine whether J.J. Ltd. should proceed with efforts to capitalize
              on U.S. opportunities
TOPIC A       U.S. role in world's economy       → evidence 1–3
TOPIC B       Attractiveness of U.S. returns     → evidence 4–6
TOPIC C       Barriers to entry                  → evidence 7–8
SUMMARY OF CONCLUSIONS  A, B, C
RECOMMENDATION          PROCEED!
```
> "Chances are that it took you at least to the summary of conclusions before you knew for
> certain the action called for. In the full presentation, that would be **at least 45 minutes,
> and maybe a lot longer.**"

**Answer-first order:**
```
RECOMMENDATION          J.J. Ltd. should proceed with efforts to capitalize on U.S. opportunities
PREVIEW OF CONCLUSIONS  A. The United States is the world's leading economy
                        B. U.S. industry returns are attractive
                        C. Barriers to entry can be overcome
CONCLUSION A            → evidence 1–3
CONCLUSION B            → evidence 4–6
CONCLUSION C            → evidence 7–8
```

The diagnosis of why teams default to the wrong one:
> "When the time comes to report on our projects, we have a tendency to **re-create the
> problem-solving approach we used** — be it the chronology of events we followed, or the
> sequence of analyses we used."

Dave McKinsey tip 31 says the same thing in one line: *"Avoid presenting the random walk you
followed in your research process."*

## 2. The message, first

Before any structure, Zelazny's message test — the **"one minute"** exercise:

> You have four hours booked. You arrive and are told you have one minute. After the little
> voice in your head finishes swearing, what would you say in the remaining 30 seconds that
> summarizes the four-hour presentation? **That's your message.**

> "Determining your message calls for a **bumper sticker mentality**… You want a message that's
> like a newspaper headline — a statement that captures your interest and makes you want to
> read more."

Business form: *"To counter the limited potential for growth at home, J.J. Ltd. should proceed
with its efforts to tap the significant growth opportunities in the United States."* — the
what's-so and the so-what in one minute.

**Zelazny's own single best tip:** open with *"If I had only 1 minute of your time today, this
is what I'd like you to get from my presentation…"*, deliver the message, then *"Fortunately,
you've given me 4 hours, so for the next 3 hours and 59 minutes, I'll give you the full
picture."*

Knaflic's equivalents: the **3-minute story** and the **Big Idea**. The Big Idea (after Duarte)
has three components: (1) it must articulate your unique point of view; (2) it must convey
what's at stake; (3) it must be a complete sentence.

## 3. The standard deck shape

mbb-deck's 10–15 slide skeleton:

| # | Slide | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title** | Document title, client/department, sub-headline, date, team, internal-vs-external |
| 2 | **Executive summary** | Governing thought + 3–5 supporting arguments, one sentence each. The whole deck on one slide. |
| 3–4 | **Context / situation** | 1–2 slides |
| 5–6 | **Complication / problem framing** | 1–2 slides |
| 7 | **Approach / framework** | How the analysis was structured — 2×2, value chain, or MECE breakdown |
| 8–12 | **Findings** | 3–5 slides, **one finding per slide**, each laddering to the governing thought |
| 13 | **Recommendation** | Restate the answer, now with full backing |
| 14 | **Implementation / next steps** | Roadmap, owners, timeline, milestones |
| 15+ | **Appendix** | Detailed data and methodology |

kgraph57's structural arc: `cover → agenda → content (with section_divider at each act
boundary) → closing → end_cover`. Its rule: *"a section_divider with no corresponding agenda
entry, or a closing with no cover, reads as an unfinished deck rather than a deliberate one."*

### Length guidance
| Source | Guidance |
|---|---|
| mbb-deck | 10–15 slides for a standard deck |
| likaku | Standard report 10–12; short 6–8; minimum 8 for a substantive topic; ~1 min/slide |
| Zelazny | **Don't ration slides by time.** *"It takes the same amount of time to present one idea on each of five visuals as it does to present five ideas on one visual."* Length is driven by the number and complexity of ideas, not the slide count. |

Zelazny's point is the important one and it cuts against slide-count budgets: if a slide budget
forces two ideas onto one slide, you have not saved time — you have broken the one-message rule
for nothing.

## 4. Storyboarding — Zelazny's six steps

The bridge between the storyline and the built deck.

> "The virtue of the tool is that it minimizes our tendency to get so wrapped up in the
> intricacies of a single chart that we fail to relate the visual to the total story."

1. **Draft the outline** of the presentation, following the story line you decided on, so you
   know what you're going to present and in what order.
2. **Decide where visuals are needed** and what those visuals might look like. (A text visual to
   introduce the structure; a pie for finding 1; a bar for finding 2; a column for finding 3.)
3. **Create the visuals.** To the right of, or underneath, each visual write out **what you're
   going to say** when you present it — including how to read the visual and its **"so what"**.
4. **Check the flow.** Test the logic of the sequence; find where there are too many charts for
   a simple point and not enough for a complicated one; determine where you need to summarize
   before moving on.
5. **Keep your medium in mind** and note any animations.
6. **Indicate the oral transition** at the bottom of each page — what you'll say that leads from
   one visual to the next.

What a storyboard buys you, per Zelazny: it *"allows us to identify transition problems"* and
*"helps us determine where we should summarize before introducing the next thought"* — two
things you cannot see from inside a single slide.

**Step 6 is the one most implementations skip**, and it's the one that makes the deck flow. An
explicit written transition per slide is the same discipline as the connective test, recorded as
an artifact rather than checked as a rule.

### McKinsey's version: keep it visible
> "Many teams find that posting the storyline or **storyboard on the team-room wall** helps keep
> the thinking focused. It also helps in bringing the client along."

## 5. Packaging: the medium shapes the storyline

Stanley & Castles, ch.4 — the "Deliver" phase.

Options: email · formal speech · paper · presentation pack · verbal briefing · workshop.
*"The key is to ensure the medium you choose makes it easy for the audience to follow your
storyline."*

Guidelines that hold across media:
- **Include an introduction so the communication can be understood by someone "not in the
  room."** (The only exception: mid-thread email where context is established.)
- **Talk through the "So what" and the top level of the story up front.** Taken with the context
  and trigger, this is the summary for busy executives. *"Doing this is a game changer — it puts
  you in charge of the story while also enabling the audience to quickly decide what they want
  to know more about."*
- **Use the format to signpost the storyline** — trackers in a pack; heading hierarchy in prose;
  bold or dot points in a short email.
- **Use visuals well** — work out your message first, then ensure visuals support it.
- **Don't be afraid of PowerPoint, but use it well.** *"Some executives have even banned it. But
  we don't think PowerPoint is the problem — it's how it's used."*

### Medium changes the structure, not just the packaging
> "If, for example, you're working on a major government report with an expectation that it
> might be 100-plus pages long… it may be better to structure it as a **grouping of separate
> recommendations rather than a single deductive storyline**. A grouping would be easier for the
> reader to absorb, rather than a deductive storyline where the reader must absorb the major and
> minor premise sections before reaching the final 'therefore' section."

So: **long document → prefer grouping. One meeting, one shot → deductive is available.**

### The one-pager may be enough
> "Be open to the idea that the one-page storyline may be enough on its own, or perhaps
> supported by a couple of data pages. Even if you initially think you need to prepare a 12- or
> 15-page data pack, once you have developed your storyline you may find that the one-pager is
> enough."

Their worked case: an executive projected a one-page storyline, clicked through context and
trigger, then the So what, then the top line, then each supporting set, finishing with the
action. Five minutes. The leadership team applauded — *"they loved that she had presented a
clear point of view without going blow by blow through the data."*

## 6. Delivering the storyline

Four essentials from Stanley & Castles:

1. **Confirm your stakeholder management strategy.** For high-stakes communication you should
   already have walked the high-level storyline past key stakeholders. Listen for agenda and
   politics; flush out the questions you'll be asked.
   *Their illustrative case:* a senior banker routinely checks with the company secretary where
   his item falls in the board's running order, who presents before and after, and on what.
   He rarely changes the substance — but if he follows a highly technical item, he double-checks
   that his explanations are easy to follow; if he follows another large funding request, he
   adjusts how he opens.
2. **Practise your delivery and your Q&A.** Scale the effort to the stakes. For a challenging
   audience, rehearse with colleagues role-playing the difficult characters — let them
   interrupt and try to throw you.
3. **Communicate your storyline sequentially.** Start at the beginning; work through step by
   step.
4. **Be open to the one-pager being enough.**

Then: **get feedback.** Ask a few people in the days after (not immediately, not too late) what
they liked and what they'd change — style, Q&A handling, the logic, the visuals. *"Don't be
afraid to ask them what they remember about your key messages"* — especially after a board or
steering committee, where yours was one of many items that day.

## 7. Speaker notes

The storyboard's "what I'll say" column becomes the speaker notes. Two conventions:
- Include **how to read the visual** and **its so-what** (Zelazny step 3)
- Include the **oral transition** to the next slide (Zelazny step 6)

pstack-loki's formulation: *speaker notes = the voiceover.* mbb-deck offers a `chart-presenter`
agent that generates a McCandless-method talking script per data slide (introduce → answer
questions → state insight → call out data → close).

## 8. Where deck and document diverge

The corpus repeatedly distinguishes **a deck to be presented** from **a document to be read**,
and the same storyline serves both with different packaging:

| | Presented | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Slide type | Key Message | Detail |
| Density | Minimal | Purposeful, dense |
| Exec summary | Contested (see below) | Definitely, at the front |
| Notes | Carry the argument | Not present — the page must argue alone |
| Type floor | 14–16pt | 8–10pt |

Dave McKinsey tip 33 argues **against** an executive summary at the front of a *presented* deck
(it's tedious to read aloud, raises more questions than can be answered, and once delivered
"you might as well end the meeting"), while allowing it in two cases: **a presentation designed
for reading**, and **as the last slide** as a recap. mbb-deck insists it belongs at slide 2.

Both are right about different artifacts. See `05-conventions/contested-points.md`.
