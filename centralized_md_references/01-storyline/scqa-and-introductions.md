# SCQA, SCR, CTQ — Introductions That Earn the Answer

Sources: Minto ch.2 & ch.4; McKinsey Staff Paper No. 66 ("Try SCR"); Stanley & Castles ch.2
(CTQ); Dave McKinsey ch.2 (SCR framework applied to real decks).

## 1. The frameworks are the same framework

| Name | Elements | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| **SCQA** | Situation → Complication → Question → Answer | Minto (canonical) |
| **SCR** | Situation → Complication → Resolution | McKinsey internal; Q folded into C |
| **SCQR** | Situation → Complication → Question → Resolution | Common hybrid label |
| **CTQ** | Context → Trigger → Question | Stanley & Castles; renames S and C for clarity |

They map 1:1. Stanley & Castles renamed "complication" to **trigger** deliberately:

> "The trigger describes what changed that has led you to communicate with your audience.
> Simply put, it's the reason for communicating. **It's not necessarily a negative factor — it
> could be an opportunity.**"

That footnote matters. Teams routinely manufacture a crisis because "complication" sounds like
one. The real question is *what changed such that we are talking now.*

All of it descends from Aristotle's *Poetics*: situation, complication, resolution.

## 2. The critical structural point most implementations get wrong

**SCQA is the introduction. It is not the deck.**

In Minto, SCQA occupies the *top* of the pyramid — it frames the question and delivers the
answer. Everything after is the pyramid body: governing thought → Key Line → support, ordered
deductively, chronologically, structurally or comparatively.

A deck that runs "Situation slides → Complication slides → Question slides → Answer slides" as
its top-level architecture has turned a three-paragraph introduction into a 20-slide dramatic
arc, and buried the answer at the back. That is the exact failure Zelazny's Shirley letter
mocks and Minto's whole book exists to prevent.

> **Rule: SCQA sizes to a paragraph, a slide, or at most the first two slides. The pyramid
> carries the rest.**

Exception worth knowing: Dave McKinsey's teardowns show real firm decks that *do* run
S/C/R as major sections — but in those decks the *presentation title itself* carries the
answer ("Restoring the USPS to Profitability by 2020"), so the resolution is never actually
withheld. The arc organizes evidence; it does not hide the conclusion.

## 3. Writing each element

### Situation / Context
The commonly agreed starting point. **The audience should simply nod.** No controversy, no
claim they'd want to argue with, nothing that needs proving.

Minto's method: take your subject, move *up* to the first non-controversial statement you can
make about it. That's your situation.

Failure modes:
- Starting too far back ("Since the invention of banking…").
- Smuggling contested claims in — anything the audience might dispute belongs in the body,
  not the intro. Stanley & Castles: *"be careful not to introduce material that will require
  justification."*
- Being generic. Compare: *"Quambatook customers have been complaining of slow delivery"*
  versus *"customers in Quambatook must currently wait five to seven days to receive their
  order even though the Mallee distribution centre is only two hours away."* The second is a
  context; the first is a shrug.

### Complication / Trigger
What changed. Why this conversation, now. Often reads as *"However…"* or *"But…"*.

It must connect tightly to the situation and lead inevitably to one question. Stanley &
Castles' worked failure:

> **Context:** SurfCo is Sydney's oldest surfboard manufacturer, historically manufacturing
> 45% of surfboards available globally.
> **Trigger:** However, our market share is falling, and last financial year we manufactured
> only 40% of boards sold globally. *Our research suggests that consumers are now asking for
> more features.*
> **Question:** How can we regain our market share?

The second trigger sentence arrives from nowhere and doesn't drive the question. Repaired:

> **Context:** Last financial year SurfCo saw increased competition cut our share of global
> surfboard sales from 45% to 40%, which we want to regain.
> **Trigger:** Our research suggests that the fall in both sales and market share stems from
> competition from new manufacturers aggressively promoting innovatively designed boards.
> **Question:** How can we compete with these new players to regain our market share?

### Question
**The single most important question you want the audience to ask** — the one that flows
naturally out of C, and that your Answer answers exactly.

- Usually left unstated in the delivered document; the audience asks it themselves.
- **Always written down explicitly during preparation.** Minto: *"you should absolutely spell
  it out for yourself before you begin to write. Otherwise, you run the danger of not being
  sure of your question."*
- Orient it from the **audience's** side. Stanley & Castles' cautionary case: a team pitched
  "Why should we partner with this prospect?" when the question was "Why should this prospect
  partner with us?" The pitch failed on that alone.
- A question that has to be split ("why *and* how") means you need a deductive storyline, not
  two questions. See `seven-storyline-patterns.md`.

### Answer / Resolution / "So what"
The governing thought. One sentence. See `02-action-titles/action-title-craft.md` for the
craft; the structural requirements are:

Stanley & Castles' five tests — the "So what" must:
1. **Answer the audience's question** — precisely; the connection must be tight.
2. **Unify the whole story** — which forces you to cull disconnected ideas.
3. **Be one idea, in one succinct sentence** — no cheating with a conjunction.
4. **Synthesise or summarise the supporting ideas** — being one idea isn't enough.
5. **Be powerful and supportable** — it must add value, not state the obvious.

Their worked climb, weakest to strongest (the SurfCo safety-audit scenario):
- *"We should be concerned about meeting the new standards."* — obvious, not insightful.
- *"We should take steps to meet the new standards."* — still obvious.
- *"It's possible to change our manufacturing processes to meet the new standards."* — implies
  our processes are sub-par or the standards unrealistic; says nothing useful.
- **"SurfCo should offer $500,000 for 51% of the Swell Boards start-up to regain market share
  from competitors within the next two years."** — specific, actioned, quantified, time-bound.

Compare the weak version of that last one: *"Offer $500,000 for a share of the Swell Boards
start-up."* Incomplete, no owner, no why, no horizon.

## 4. Order matters, and it's not arbitrary

Minto's ordering (S then C then Q) exists because the reader must be standing on stable ground
before you disturb it. Reversing — leading with the complication — can work for a very short,
very familiar audience, but it costs you the nod.

## 5. Minto's four common introduction patterns

Nearly every business document answers one of four questions. Knowing which you're in tells
you what the Key Line has to contain.

| Pattern | Question | Key Line contains |
|---|---|---|
| **Giving direction** | What should we do? / How should we do it? | **Steps.** ("How?" always answers with steps.) |
| **Seeking approval to spend money** | Should we do it? | Reasons it's worth it + affordability + returns |
| **Explaining "how to"** | How should we do it? | Steps in the process |
| **Choosing among alternatives** | What should we do? | Criteria → options assessed → the pick |

Two consulting-specific patterns Minto adds: **letters of proposal** and **progress reviews**.

**The directive pattern is worth internalizing** because it inverts the usual assumption: in a
directive you are *planting* the question in the reader's mind rather than reminding them of
one they already have. Minto's skeleton:

```
S = We want to do X
C = We need you to do Y
Q = (How do I do Y?)
A = Steps
```

Note her observation: **the Complication and the Answer are roughly reversals of each other**,
because the answer is the effect of carrying out the actions, which by construction solves the
problem named in the complication. If your C and A don't mirror, one of them is wrong.

## 6. SCR in one paragraph — McKinsey's own worked example

> "The retail bank market in China is profitable at 15% EBIT and is rapidly growing at a 12%
> CAGR **(situation)**. However, Pioneer Bank is poorly positioned to share in this growing
> profit pool **(complication)**. Pioneer needs to acquire Shanghai Bank and invest $60 million
> in building capabilities to establish a winning position **(resolution)**."
> — McKinsey Staff Paper No. 66

And the firm's own standard for it: *"Any synthesis that does not fit into this format can
probably be made clearer and more insightful by being modified accordingly."*

## 7. Six-point checklist for a finished introduction

1. Does the audience nod at the Situation, with nothing to argue?
2. Does the Trigger say what *changed*, and is it tightly linked to the Situation?
3. Does exactly *one* Question flow from S+C — and is it the question you actually answer?
4. Is the Question framed from the audience's side, not yours?
5. Is the Answer one sentence, one idea, specific, and a genuine synthesis?
6. Could someone not in the room follow it? (Stanley & Castles: the introduction exists so the
   communication survives without you.)
