# The Pyramid Principle

Source: Barbara Minto, *The Minto Pyramid Principle*, 3rd ed. (2010), Parts 1–2; reinforced by
McKinsey Staff Paper No. 66 (2007), "Synthesize findings".

Minto is the load-bearing wall. SCQA, MECE, governing thought, action titles and every
"answer first" instruction in every downstream source trace back here. Get this right and the
rest is application.

## 1. Why a pyramid at all

The reader can hold roughly seven items in working memory, and will *impose* a structure on
whatever they receive whether or not you supplied one. If you don't group and summarize, the
reader groups and summarizes — badly, and in a way you don't control.

So: **ideas are presented top-down, but discovered bottom-up.** You think by assembling facts
into groups and abstracting a point from each group. You *communicate* by stating the point
first and the facts after. Reversing the order of discovery is the whole trick.

## 2. The three rules

A structure is a valid pyramid if and only if all three hold:

> 1. **Ideas at any level must always be summaries of the ideas grouped below them.**
> 2. **Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea.**
> 3. **Ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered.**
>
> — Minto, ch.1 *(OCR-repaired)*

### Rule 1 — summary, not label
The parent states what the children *mean*, not what category they fall into. Violating this
produces the **intellectually blank assertion** (see `02-action-titles/action-title-craft.md`,
which treats this as the central title defect).

### Rule 2 — same kind of idea
> "If the first idea in a grouping is a reason for doing something, the other ideas in that
> grouping must also be reasons for doing the same thing. If the first idea is a step in a
> process, the rest of the ideas in the grouping must also be steps in the same process."

**The plural-noun test.** You must be able to label the group with one plural noun —
*reasons*, *steps*, *problems*, *changes*, *options*, *risks*. If the best you can do is
"things" or "considerations", the grouping is invalid. Apples and pears group as *fruit*;
apples and chairs only group as *objects*, which is too high to carry meaning.

### Rule 3 — logically ordered
There is a *reason* the second idea is second. Minto: there are exactly four possible orders,
because there are exactly four analytical operations the mind can perform.

| Order | Arises from | Looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Deductive** | Reasoning in successive steps | major premise → minor premise → conclusion |
| **Chronological** | Working out cause and effect | first, second, third |
| **Structural** | Dividing a whole into parts | Boston, New York, Washington |
| **Comparative** (degree) | Categorizing | most important → least important |

If you cannot name which of the four you used, you have not finished thinking. Order that
can't be named is usually a grouping that isn't MECE.

## 3. The three substructures

Every pyramid has three relationships running through it simultaneously.

### Vertical — the Q&A dialogue
Each box, when stated, raises a question in the reader's mind; the boxes beneath it answer
exactly that question. Down the pyramid: *statement → question → answer*. This is what makes
a document feel like it is talking to you rather than at you.

Practically: if a slide's title raises a question its own body doesn't answer, the vertical
logic is broken. If it raises a question the *next* slide answers, that's horizontal flow
(see `05-conventions/mbb-rules-of-the-road.md`).

### Horizontal — deduction or induction
Within a group, ideas relate to each other one of two ways, never both:

**Deductive** — successive steps. Statement about the world → statement about the subject of
that statement → the "therefore".
- Fragile: break any link and the conclusion falls.
- Demands patience: the reader must hold all premises before the payoff.
- Summarize it by leaning heavily on the final conclusion.

**Inductive** — a set of ideas of the same kind, described by one plural noun.
- Robust: lose one reason and the argument weakens but survives.
- Summarize it by stating **what the similarity implies** — this is the hard part.

> "If you choose to answer the question raised by an idea deductively, you know [the steps
> follow]; if you choose to answer inductively, you know the ideas in the grouping must be
> logically the same kind." *(OCR-repaired)*

**Practical guidance (Minto, and Stanley & Castles independently):** use deduction sparingly
and never stack it. A deductive chain hanging underneath another deductive chain is how
arguments collapse. Deduce at the top, group underneath.

### Introductory flow — the narrative
Situation → Complication → Question → Answer. Covered fully in
`01-storyline/scqa-and-introductions.md`.

## 4. The Key Line

The **Key Line** is the top row of supporting arguments — the boxes directly under the
governing thought. In a deck, the Key Line *is* the executive summary and *is* the section
structure. Minto's advice: state the Key Line points as part of the introduction, so the
reader knows the shape of the argument before descending into any leg.

Rule of thumb across sources: **2–5 Key Line points.** Stanley & Castles make it explicit —
"each supporting idea must parent two to five subordinate ideas". One is not a group; six
means you haven't found the real grouping.

## 5. Building the pyramid — the top-down procedure

Minto's preferred method, because you start with what you're most sure of:

1. **Draw the top box.** Write the *subject*.
2. **Decide the Question** you're answering for this reader about this subject.
3. **Write the Answer**, if you have it.
4. **Identify the Situation** — go up from the subject and make the first *non-controversial*
   statement you can about it. Something the reader will simply nod at.
5. **Develop the Complication** — "so what went wrong / what changed?"
6. **Re-check the Question.** The S and the C should make your Q inevitable. If they don't,
   your Q is wrong — or your S and C are.
7. **Check the Answer** still answers the Q.
8. **Find the Key Line.** Decide: answering deductively or inductively? If inductively, *what
   is your plural noun?*

Step 8's phrasing is Minto's own checklist item and is the sharpest single diagnostic in the
book. A team that cannot name the plural noun does not yet have a storyline.

The bottom-up approach (list your points, group them, abstract upward, then work out the
introduction that makes that answer relevant) is the fallback when you genuinely don't know
your answer yet.

## 6. Summarizing groups — the real work

This is Minto ch.7 and it is where most storylines fail. Two cases:

### Action ideas → state the *effect* of carrying out the actions
> "Since actions are always taken to achieve some purpose, the summary of a set of actions is
> always the effect of carrying out the actions. Any MECE set of actions plus the effect they
> produce will together form a unique closed system." *(OCR-repaired)*

Actions are indistinguishable from one another in isolation — they all read "we should
[verb]". You cannot tell whether one belongs with another *except* by reference to the effect
they jointly produce. So:

- **Word each action as specifically as possible before you try to group them.**
- Keep each grouping to five or fewer.
- Derive the effect *directly from* the wording of the actions.

**The end-product test.** State the effect so specifically that it implies something you could
hold in your hand. Not "so that I can improve profits" — a 2% and a 10% improvement are both
improvements, and they demand different actions. Say "to improve profits by 10% by January 15".

Minto's table of vague → end-product wording (Exhibit 30):

| What was said | What was meant |
|---|---|
| Strengthen regional effectiveness | Assign planning responsibility to the regions |
| Reduce accounts receivable | Establish a system for following up overdue accounts |
| Review management processes | Determine whether management processes need to be revised |
| Improve financial reporting | Install a system that gives early notice of change |
| Tackle strategic issues | Define a clear long-term strategy |
| Redeploy manpower resources | Place people in positions of comparable responsibility |

Test: **visualize a real person taking the action, and ask what they are holding when they're
done.** If nothing, the wording is gibberish — however professional it sounds.

Minto's own example of unsalvageable phrasing: *"A world consciousness must be developed
through which every individual realizes his role as a member of the world community."* You
cannot tell who has done it, so you cannot decompose it, so it has no intellectual value.

### Situation ideas → state what their similarity *implies*
Not "there are three findings" but the insight the three findings jointly deliver. Minto's
radio example: hearing *"for three reasons"* anchors nothing and the listener replies to the
first sub-point. Hearing *"…because he and Johnson are essentially the same kind of people"*
gives them the actual claim to react to.

## 7. Testing a finished pyramid

From McKinsey Staff Paper No. 66, three tests — the cleanest formulation in the corpus:

- **Going down.** Does each governing thought pose a *single* question (usually "why" *or*
  "how", not both) that is answered completely by the boxes below it?
- **Going across.** Is each level MECE?
- **Going up.** Does each group of boxes, taken together, give one answer — one "so what?" —
  that is essentially the governing thought above it?

Plus Minto's own: if any of the three rules breaks, that is not a formatting problem. It is a
signal that the thinking is incomplete, the ideas are underdeveloped, or they don't actually
relate. Fix the thinking.

## 8. What this means for a deck

- The **governing thought** is slide 2 (the executive summary), not slide 20.
- The **Key Line** points are the deck's sections, and their statements are the exec-summary
  bullets — the same sentences, verbatim.
- Each **section** is one leg of the pyramid; each slide in it is a supporting idea.
- Each **slide title** is a summary of that slide's body (rule 1 applied at the leaf).
- Reading titles top to bottom reconstructs the pyramid. That is *why* the title-ladder read
  works as a test — it isn't a stylistic nicety, it's a structural assertion.
