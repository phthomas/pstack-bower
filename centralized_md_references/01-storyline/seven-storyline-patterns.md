# The Seven Classic Business Storyline Patterns

Source: Davina Stanley & Gerard Castles, *The So What Strategy* (Revised ed., 2017), ch.2–3.

This is the highest-leverage reference in the library. Minto tells you the *rules* a storyline
must obey; this tells you the *shapes* storylines actually take. Picking a pattern first and
populating it is faster and more reliable than building from a blank page — and it is the
piece missing from every prior-art skill surveyed.

> "People often talk about the seven great story structures… Well, in business we think there
> are also underlying patterns in business communication."

## Choosing: the one question that decides everything

```
Does the audience need to know WHY (or HOW, or WHAT) — one of them?
        │
        ├── YES, only one  ──────────────►  GROUPING structure
        │                                   (Action Jackson · The Pitch · Traffic Light)
        │
        └── They need BOTH why AND how  ──►  DEDUCTIVE structure
                                            (Close the Gap · Houston · To B or Not to B · Watch Out)
```

Restated: **a grouping structure has room to discuss either why or how at the top level, but
not both.** A deductive structure wraps why and how into one journey.

Secondary selector, once you know the family:

| Situation | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Already agreed; just need the plan | **Action Jackson** |
| Need to sell an idea; no plan needed yet | **The Pitch** |
| Reporting status | **Traffic Light** |
| Known target, we fall short, here's how we close it | **Close the Gap** |
| Problem they may not accept, plus its cause, plus the fix | **Houston, We Have a Problem** |
| Several options; recommend one | **To B or Not to B** |
| Things are going well, but risks are emerging | **Watch Out** |

**Warning from the authors:** *"Do not force fit your thinking into the pattern and sacrifice
its logical integrity. If one of the patterns is not right, you may need to look for another
one or potentially start building your storyline from scratch."*

# Part A — Grouping storylines

Shape: **So what** on top, 2–5 parallel supporting ideas below, each supported in turn.
Robust — lose one leg and the argument bends but holds.

## 1. Action Jackson — for action plans

**Use when:** the audience already agrees action is needed. You're only explaining *what steps*.

**Shape:**
```
CTQ — reminds them of what was already agreed; leads to a HOW question
So what — a statement: "We should do X" or "We are implementing a 3-month plan to deliver Y"
  ├─ Step 1  (starts with a verb)
  ├─ Step 2
  └─ Step 3
```

**Rules:**
- The introduction must lead to a **"how"** question, not a "why" question.
- The So what is a **statement** — a recommendation or a statement of what's agreed.
- **Every supporting idea is an action, ideally starting with a verb.**

**Do not use when** the audience still needs convincing. If they do, you want The Pitch (why
only), or Close the Gap / Houston (why then how).

**Real examples:** a bank finance team explaining how it will close an already-acknowledged
stretch-target shortfall; a team briefing a senior exec on the deployment plan for a new
system, delivered verbally off a one-pager.

---

## 2. The Pitch — for pitches and proposals

**Use when:** you must persuade someone *why*. Do not explain how before they want the what.

> "An often-quoted maxim is that why is more important than how. If you have not persuaded
> your audience why something should be done then don't tell them how to do it."

**Shape:**
```
CTQ — the context very often describes THE PROBLEM YOU ARE THERE TO SOLVE
So what — your value proposition, stated so they trust you can fix it
  ├─ Reason 1  (the one that benefits THEM most — first)
  ├─ Reason 2
  └─ Reason 3     (three to five reasons, MECE from the audience's perspective)
```

**Rules:**
- Sweat the purpose and the audience even though neither appears on the page.
- **Sweat the introduction even harder.** The context should name their problem concretely.
- The So what must give them confidence you know exactly how to help.
- 3–5 reasons, **MECE from the audience's perspective**, ordered with their biggest benefit
  first.

**The trap: glibness.** *"We have described The Pitch as a simple structure, but that
simplicity is deceptive."* Points must be tailored to the audience's specific concerns in both
language and substance. Explain why *they* benefit, not why you do.

**Real examples:** a tech company pitching a telco partnership against a competitor, where one
decision-maker was pre-committed to a rival network type — the team spent its energy on the
first two reasons, aimed squarely at that person's concerns. Also: pitching for a C-suite role.

## 3. Traffic Light — for updates

**Use when:** reporting status. Every update, however complex, resolves to red, amber or green.

**Shape:**
```
CTQ
So what — the overall status, nuanced (not literally one word — but it resolves to one)
  ├─ Element 1 + its status and why
  ├─ Element 2 + its status and why
  └─ Element 3 + its status and why
```

**Strength:** enormous flexibility, and it gives an *overall* judgement rather than a list of
component states. Works at macro (board/shareholder program update) and micro (team huddle).

**Three traps — the flexibility is also the weakness:**
1. **Assuming what the audience knows.** You live in the detail; they may be tracking external
   trends you've missed. Rethink the introduction each time.
2. **Cut-and-pasting from last time.** Sometimes right; never the default.
3. **Plonking down supporting ideas without regard for how they hang together.** Easy to list
   reasons; hard to be confident the set is complete and intelligently ordered.

**Real example (verbal, mine-site maintenance):**
> **Context:** We are always looking for ways to improve our maintenance program.
> **Trigger:** Working with ConsultCo over the past few weeks, we found a way to improve the
> maintenance program that will remove the bottlenecks within the mine.
> **Question:** How will that work?
> **Answer:** We are changing the way we think and communicate about maintenance tasks to cut
> the risk of downtime, such as last month's major shutdown.
> - We are now successfully prioritising maintenance tasks according to the impact on
>   production rather than the time when the task was logged.
> - We are continuing to improve communication between shift supervisors and the maintenance
>   team by ensuring they discuss priorities at the start of each shift.
> - We are working towards operators consistently telling supervisors as soon as they see a
>   potential problem rather than waiting until something breaks.

# Part B — Deductive storylines

Shape: **statement → comment → therefore.** Three top-level boxes, each doing a distinct job.
Powerful but fragile: break one link and the recommendation is irrelevant.

**Universal deductive rules (all four patterns):**
1. As a whole, the supporting ideas must lead to **only one possible recommendation**.
2. Each supporting idea must do its job as **statement**, **comment**, or **therefore**:
   - **Statement** — something new to the audience that lays the foundation. May set
     conditions to be met, define a problem, or state criteria for success.
   - **Comment** — comments on the statement, *tightly*. Usually begins "however" or "but".
     **If the connection to the statement is weak, the storyline fails.**
   - **Therefore** — the only natural action if the first two hold, and the links hold.
3. **Each supporting idea must parent a grouping** that passes MECE. Never stack a deductive
   chain under a deductive chain — it compounds fragility and exhausts the audience.
4. Each supporting idea must add genuine value. No padding.

**How storyline deduction differs from classical logic.** Classical: *Socrates is a man; all
men are mortal; therefore Socrates is mortal* — proves a fact. Storyline deduction drives to
an **action**: *all men in our group must have beards; Socrates is clean-shaven and wants to
join; therefore Socrates must grow a beard.*

**The tightness test**, from the book:

| Fails | Works |
|---|---|
| **S:** Top surfers are incredibly fit. | **S:** All top surfers are incredibly fit. |
| **C:** You are not fit. | **C:** You want to be a top surfer but are not incredibly fit. |
| **T:** Therefore, you should get fit. | **T:** Therefore, you need to become incredibly fit. |

The left column fails because S and C aren't tightly enough connected to force that T — and
it was never established that you want to be a top surfer.

## 4. Close the Gap — for improvement recommendations

**Use when:** you know what success looks like (or what compliance requires), you're not there
yet, and you need buy-in on the plan **in one meeting**.

**Shape:**
```
CTQ
So what
  ├─ STATEMENT:  Here is what success requires  (the criteria / the standard / the target)
  ├─ COMMENT:    Here is where we actually stand against it  (the gap)
  └─ THEREFORE:  Here is how we close it        (the action plan)
```

**Choose over Action Jackson when** the audience needs educating on what's required *at the
same time* as receiving the plan.

**Three failure modes the authors see constantly:**
1. **Incomplete or poorly organised criteria in the statement.** If the criteria set is
   incomplete or inaccurate, the whole story falls down.
2. **Comment not tightly linked to the statement.** The audience starts debating you and never
   reaches your action plan.
3. **Therefore doesn't obviously flow.** Natural to you ≠ natural to an outsider. Have a
   colleague check the high-level logic before you build anything.

**Real examples:** legal advice to a US company on ACCC rules for an Australian asset purchase
(criteria → position → steps), delivered as a letter plus a call. Also: the agile stand-up —
*"for the model to be delivered by Friday we need all XYZ fields complete; but three fields
corrupted in last night's test; so Mary and Jane down tools and help Fred fix them."*

Macro range: business cases, legal advice, due diligence reports, audits.

## 5. Houston, We Have a Problem — for explaining how to solve problems

**Use when:** you must convince a possibly sceptical audience that a problem is real *and*
walk them through the fix, in one sitting.

**Shape:**
```
CTQ
So what — a recommendation that ALSO includes why it's the right one
  ├─ STATEMENT:  The problem, and that it is serious enough to warrant attention
  ├─ COMMENT:    THE cause (or set of causes) of that problem
  └─ THEREFORE:  Actions — which must address the causes named in the comment
```

**The So what is distinctive here:** it must carry both the what and the why. The book's
example — *"We need Marketing to provide the latest sales data to finish the model"* — states
the action (get the data) and the reason (to finish the model). *Either half alone is not
sufficient for a Houston So what.*

**Three traps:**
1. **Don't use it if they already know the problem and its cause.** *"Don't waste time telling
   me what I already know."* Boring and frustrating.
2. **Avoid it if your audience *is* the cause.** Inflaming or embarrassing stakeholders rarely
   helps. There's always another way to tell the story.
3. **The cause must be *the* cause,** not *a* cause. If it's only one contributor, the
   "therefore" isn't the only sensible path.

**Real example:** a bank finance team explaining a revenue shortfall to a BU leadership team —
significance of the shortfall, then its causes (hard news for specific product owners,
pre-briefed), then the fix across all four product areas with focus on the two underperforming
ones.

## 6. To B or Not to B — for explaining which option is best

**Use when:** options must be assessed and one recommended, and the audience doesn't already
know the landscape.

> "Too often we see leaders asserting that everyone should follow the option they are
> recommending, without explaining why it's the right option. They just say 'follow me'."

**Shape:**
```
CTQ — sets up the story; introduces nothing that itself needs justifying
So what — a recommendation that also explains WHY this solution should be adopted
  ├─ STATEMENT:  Here are the options available  (complete, mutually exclusive)
  ├─ COMMENT:    Assessed against consistent criteria, B is best
  └─ THEREFORE:  Here is what's required to implement B
```

**Rules:**
- **Explain first, recommend last** — within the body. (The So what still leads.)
- Don't fall into the trap of a So what that merely says *"There are three options."* That's an
  intellectually blank assertion (see Minto).
- **The option set must be complete and mutually exclusive.** Once the audience accepts there's
  a problem worth solving, that's exactly where their mind goes. Every option gets analysed
  against the *same* criteria.
- Only use it if they *don't* already know the problem details — otherwise you're annoying them.

**Real examples:** an IT team winning COO approval for a cloud data-management vendor after
previously losing support by over-teching the pitch — three providers, one criteria set,
"Black" wins, then a high-level plan. Result: *"if they presented business cases like that in
the future he'd approve everything — fast."* Also: outsource-vs-insource for an
underperforming vendor, presented to a steering committee containing the person who had
originally recommended that vendor.

**Worked full example** (Appendix A of the book, converted to a prose memo):
> Coonawarra Corp should invest in Black's intelligent cloud-based storage solution
> - We investigated four options to deliver intelligent cloud-based data storage capabilities.
> - Of the four options, Black Inc. provides the best solution for our needs.
> - As a result, we recommend undertaking a 10-week program to initiate the migration to Black.

Note the third-level structure under option assessment — each option gets a *verdict* title,
not a label: *"Option 1 – Outsourcing to Yellow Company is possible but risky"*, *"Option 2 –
Purchasing tools and technology from Pink Corporation is overly complicated"*, *"Option 4 –
Outsourcing to Black Inc. meets all criteria"*. This is action-title discipline applied at
depth.

## 7. Watch Out — to counter emerging risks

**Use when:** things are broadly going well, but risks ahead must be managed and you need the
audience to act.

**Shape:**
```
CTQ — the question must be about ACTION, not just an update
So what — a recommendation for action that includes why it's the right one
  ├─ STATEMENT:  What's been working / the wins so far
  ├─ COMMENT:    Risks are emerging that MUST be managed for the overall task to succeed
  └─ THEREFORE:  Actions to address those risks
```

**The comment is the whole pattern.** Without the imperative — *these risks, unmanaged, break
the outcome* — they're just "any old risks" and no action follows.

> "Don't fall into the trap of just laying out what's working, what's not, and actions. A flow
> of ideas that simply relate to the same topic is not sufficiently compelling."

**Real examples:** a project leader making the case to a steering committee mid-portfolio-
review; a team member giving a leadership "heads up" — FY17 wins, FY18 concerns serious enough
to warrant action, then gently recommended actions. (She'd normally have brought spreadsheets;
she used the one-page storyline instead and got applause.)
