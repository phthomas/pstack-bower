# Storyline Quality Tests

Every explicit, runnable test for a storyline found in the corpus, gathered in one place. These
are the raw material for gates and linters.

## 1. Stanley & Castles — structural rules by family

### Five rules for logical grouping storylines
1. **Together, the supporting ideas must pass the MECE test.** (The book's mnemonic for people
   who dislike the acronym: **NONG** — *no overlaps, no gaps*.)
2. **Each supporting idea must provoke and respond to just one question in the audience's
   mind.** (You say "we should have dinner Saturday"; they ask "where?" — you don't then list
   reasons to eat out.)
3. **Each supporting idea must synthesise or summarise the ideas under it.**
   - *Summary* describes a set of data: "All Australian surfboards are great to surf with."
   - *Synthesis* explains what the set means: "When choosing a new surfboard, you must choose
     an Australian board as they are the best."
4. **Each supporting idea must parent two to five subordinate ideas.** A long list means you
   haven't found the theme. One child is not a group.
5. **Each supporting idea must add genuine value.** Be wary of content that only demonstrates
   how much work was done. *Be ruthless.*

### Four rules for deductive storylines
1. As a whole, the supporting ideas must lead to **only one possible recommendation**.
2. Each supporting idea must do its job as **statement**, **comment** or **therefore**.
3. Each supporting idea must **parent a grouping** that passes MECE — never another deductive
   chain.
4. Each supporting idea must add genuine value.

### Five rules for the "So what"
It must:
1. Answer your audience's question — precisely; the connection must be tight.
2. Unify your whole story — forcing you to cull disconnected ideas.
3. Be one idea, framed as one succinct sentence — check it really is just one idea.
4. Synthesise or summarise the supporting ideas.
5. Be powerful and supportable — it must add value.

### The 10-Point Storyline Test
The book presents a 10-point test tying all the rules together, with this calibration:

> "A score of less than 7 out of 10 on this test would indicate that more work must be done,
> and you should really be aiming for a 10 out of 10. Try this on one of your storylines to
> test how strong it is. Or, better still, **ask someone else to test your storyline.**"

The ten points are the union of the rules above, applied as: introduction (4 checks —
context nods, trigger states change, one question flows, question is audience-oriented), the
So what (5 checks above), and the supporting structure (MECE, one-question-each, synthesis,
2–5 children, genuine value). *The book presents the scored list as an exhibit image; the
constituent rules are fully reproduced above.*

### The four criteria a strong storyline must meet
From ch.1:
1. The **introduction must be right** — flows naturally, relevant, interesting, zeroes in on
   the right question.
2. The storyline must have **one clear, overarching idea** — the "So what".
3. *(supporting structure must be logically sound)*
4. *(each level must synthesise the one below)*

## 2. Minto — the three rules, as tests

1. Is every parent a **summary** of its children (not a label for them)?
2. Is every group **the same kind of idea** — nameable with one plural noun?
3. Is every group **logically ordered** by one of exactly four orders (deductive,
   chronological, structural, comparative)? Can you *name* which?

Plus the **end-product test** for action statements: visualize a real person completing the
action — what are they holding? If nothing, rewrite.

And the **intellectually blank assertion** test: does the statement say what the ideas *mean*,
or only what *kind* of idea follows? "There are two problems in the organization" is blank.

## 3. McKinsey Staff Paper No. 66 — the three pyramid tests

- **Going down.** Does each governing thought pose a single question (usually "why" *or* "how",
  but not both) that is answered *completely* by the group of boxes below it?
- **Going across.** Is each level within the pyramid MECE?
- **Going up.** Does each group of boxes, taken together, provide one answer — one "so what?" —
  that is essentially the governing thought above it?

Plus, on the governing thought itself:
> "If you were to articulate a governing thought to a client and the client's first reaction
> were to ask what the company should do, you would know that your governing thought was
> incomplete."

And on hypotheses (which become storyline claims):
- Can it be **proven right or wrong**?
- Is it **not obvious**?
- Does it **point directly to an action** the client can take?
- *"If you shared your hypothesis with the CEO, would it sound naive or obvious?"*

**Test the solution:** what would it mean if all your hypotheses came true? (The paper's
cautionary tale: a team at a regulated utility had a perfect hypothesis set whose combined
implication was untenable.)

## 4. The horizontal-logic tests (title ladder)

### The memo / ladder read
Strip everything but the titles. Read top to bottom. Does the sequence argue, on its own,
without any slide bodies? *(mbb-deck; pstack-loki; Dave McKinsey tip 51 — "If you are pinched
for time, you can read through the titles only, one after the other, and still give the
complete picture.")*

### The connective test
Between every pair of consecutive titles you must be able to insert **"therefore"** or
**"but"** — never **"and then"**. "And then" means the slides are adjacent, not connected.
*(pstack-loki; derived from the Parker/Stone but–therefore rule.)*

### The question-chain test
> "To 'own the flow,' each slide should trigger a question answered by the title of the next
> slide." — Dave McKinsey, tip 17

Worked: title *"USPS is experiencing unprecedented losses"* → reader asks *"why?"* → next title
*"Losses have been driven by volume declines, RHB pre-funding requirements, and limitations on
cost savings."*

This is the strongest of the three because it is *diagnostic*: it tells you not just that the
flow is broken but what the missing slide should say. It is Minto's vertical Q&A dialogue
applied horizontally across a deck.

### The "so what?" chain
For every slide, ask "so what?" The next slide's title should answer it. *(mbb-deck)*

## 5. Slide-level tests

- **Vertical logic.** Does the slide body fully support — and *unambiguously prove* — its
  title? *(mbb-deck principle 9; Dave McKinsey tip 40)*
- **One message.** Can the slide be summarized in a single sentence? If not, split it.
- **Title/body number match.** Every number in the title must appear, identically, in the body.
- **10-second executive test.** Can a senior executive get the point in 10 seconds? Does the
  title tell them what to think? Could any element be removed without losing the message?
- **90-second rule.** If a slide takes more than 90 seconds to read, it's too dense.
- **Squint test / glance test.** Squint at it — the most important element must still be
  obvious. BCG's version: *"Which will help your audience understand your main point at a
  single glance?"*

## 6. Deck-level tests

From kgraph57's rubric, the cleanest deck-level formulation:
- Do the headlines alone form **one logical argument** — a pyramid, governing thought supported
  by grouped reasons?
- Is each headline **a single proposition** — one answer or one tension, not multiple claims
  joined by "and"?
- Does any slide's headline **repeat or contradict** another's?

> "If any answer fails, fix the storyline before polishing individual slides."

## 7. Testing socially, not just mechanically

Every source that discusses this converges on the same point: **have someone else test it.**

- Stanley & Castles: *"ask someone else to test your storyline"*; and for deductive patterns,
  *"we always recommend you ask a colleague to review your logic at a high level before you
  prepare your more detailed communication."*
- McKinsey SP66: keep the emerging storyline **visible** on the team-room wall.
- Stanley & Castles again: rehearse with colleagues role-playing the difficult stakeholders,
  scaled to the stakes.

Implication for a skill: the storyline gate should feel like a *review sitting*, and the
critique should come from something with fresh eyes — not from the same context that wrote it.

## 8. What order to run tests in

Cheapest-and-most-structural first, because a failure upstream invalidates everything below:

```
1. Purpose + audience stated            (Stanley & Castles: "As a result of my
                                         communication, I want my audience to…")
2. Introduction (CTQ) passes            → 6-point checklist
3. So what passes                       → 5 rules
4. Pattern chosen and honestly fits     → 7 patterns
5. Structure passes                     → grouping (5 rules) or deductive (4 rules)
6. Pyramid tests                        → down / across / up
7. Title ladder                         → memo read, connective, question-chain
8. Per-slide                            → vertical logic, one message, number match
9. Rendered deck                        → squint, 90-second, glance
```

Fixes route **upstream**: a flow problem is a storyline defect, not a slide defect.
