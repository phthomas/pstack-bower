# Hypothesis-Driven Problem Solving

Source: *The McKinsey Approach to Problem Solving*, Staff Paper No. 66 (Davis, Keeling,
Schreier & Williams, July 2007); Minto Part 3; Dave McKinsey tips 1–7.

A deck is the output of a problem-solving process. When the thinking upstream is weak, no
amount of storyline craft rescues it — the storyline just makes the weakness legible. This
reference covers where the *content* comes from.

## 1. Rigorous problem definition

> "Many 'bad' engagements are rooted in a lack of common understanding of success."

### The Problem Statement Worksheet

```
                    BASIC QUESTION TO BE RESOLVED
    Must be SMART: specific, measurable, action-oriented, relevant, time-bound
    e.g. "How can Pioneer Bank close the $100 million profitability gap in two years?"

 1. CONTEXT                          4. CONSTRAINTS WITHIN SOLUTION SPACE
    Situation and complication          Limits on solutions considered
    facing the client — industry        — e.g. must be organic, not
    trends, relative position           inorganic, growth

 2. CRITERIA FOR SUCCESS             5. STAKEHOLDERS
    Shared by client and team;          Who decides, who can help,
    quantitative AND qualitative        who can block
    — impact, timing, visibility,
    mindset shifts

 3. SCOPE OF SOLUTION SPACE          6. KEY SOURCES OF INSIGHT
    What's in and what's out            Where expertise and prior
                                        knowledge already exist
```

Notes from the paper:
- **Sharpen scope by asking what is *not* in scope.** Then define constraints. Don't get bogged
  down distinguishing the two.
- Success criteria must include the qualitative: capability building, mindset shifts, timing
  and visibility of impact — not just the number.
- The problem definition should go **beyond analysis** to encompass capability building,
  changing mindsets and implementation. A client asking "which market should we enter?" may
  fail to ask what capabilities they need to build to enter it — *"but these must nevertheless
  be part of the answer."*

An EM quoted in the paper: *"Any time I have not been clear about an element of the Problem
Statement Worksheet at the start of a study with the client, it always results in scope creep."*

### SMART, applied to the deck title
Dave McKinsey (tips 8–9) carries SMART up into the presentation title itself:

> "Doubling Serbia's Economic Growth in the Next Decade" — **specific** (Serbia's economic
> growth), **measurable** (doubling), **action-oriented** (the present participle), **relevant**
> (to the audience), **time-bound** ("the next decade").

And his critique of a real deck: McKinsey titled its USPS work *"USPS Future Business Model"* —
a noun phrase, a "what". Applying the firm's own practice would have given *"Restoring the USPS
to Profitability by 2020."*

**The –ing verb convention.** Real McKinsey deck titles consistently open with a present
participle: *Sustaining Quality and Operational Excellence*; *Capturing the Full Electricity
Efficiency Potential of the U.K.*; *Evolving USSA's Alpine Domestic Development System in
Partnership with Clubs.* The present participle signals action still in progress and gets
action-orientation for free.

## 2. Structuring the problem

Two tools. Use the hypothesis tree when you know enough to guess; the issue tree when you don't.

### Issue trees
Disaggregate the primary question into a cascade of *questions* that, once answered, together
answer the primary question.

> Primary: "How can Pioneer Bank grow at least $100 million over the next two years?"
> → "What is the currently anticipated organic growth?"
> → "What additional organic growth can potentially be stimulated?"
> → "How much growth can be captured through acquisitions?"

### Hypothesis trees
The same shape, but each node is a *decisive answer* rather than a question. Convert by
rewriting: "How can we improve profitability?" becomes "We can improve profitability by…";
"How can we lower total cost?" becomes "We can lower total cost by…".

> "Hypothesis trees use the minimum necessary number of hypotheses to drive insight."

**Every hypothesis must:**
- Be **provable right or wrong**
- Be **not obvious**
- **Point directly to an action** the client can take

Worked example from the paper:
```
Pioneer Bank can          ├─ Pioneer can increase selling   ├─ transfer/outsource non-value-added
increase sales-force      │  time as a proportion of        │  tasks (e.g. administration)
productivity              │  total available time           └─ reduce/eliminate non-value-added
                          │                                    tasks (e.g. travel, error correction)
                          └─ Pioneer can increase sales     ├─ improve generation of sales leads
                             volumes from available         └─ increase proportion of leads
                             selling time                      converted to sales
```

**The honest caveat, in the firm's own words:** the hypothesis approach *"can appear premature
and arrogant to clients"*, and *"can indeed trap us in a limiting frame of mind, reinforcing
beliefs that are not justified by the facts and reducing the opportunity for an 'outside the
box' solution."* Great problem solvers *"not only seek information that confirms their
hypothesis but also mercilessly hunt for disconfirming information"* (Dave McKinsey).

### Minto's diagnostic frameworks (Part 3)
Where hypotheses come from, structurally:
- **Showing physical structure** — map the system, then locate the fault in it.
- **Tracing cause and effect** — follow the chain backwards from the symptom.
- **Classifying possible causes** — enumerate the categories of thing that could cause this.

Minto's problem-definition elements: **Starting Point / Opening Scene**, the **Disturbing
Event**, **R1** (undesired result — where you are), **R2** (desired result — where you want to
be). The question is always some form of "how do we get from R1 to R2?", and *this converts
directly into the SCQA introduction.* That conversion is the bridge between problem solving and
communication.

## 3. The dot-dash storyline — the Day 1 answer

The most under-appreciated artifact in the corpus, and the one most worth stealing.

> "While this hierarchy can be laid out as a tree… the best problem solvers capture it by
> creating **dot-dash storylines**."
>
> "Forcing the hypotheses into storyline format — a dot-dash memo of hypotheses — typically
> drives an additional level of precision in language and logic. Done well, this dot-dash memo
> is also a simpler and more robust way of communicating — it will become the preliminary
> outline for the final progress review and will simply grow to encompass the insights that
> emerge from subsequent analysis. Teams often call this the 'Day 1' or 'Week 1' answer or
> draft storyline. **We strongly recommend this technique.**"

Form: nested dots and dashes — a plain-text outline where each line is a full declarative
sentence, indentation carries the pyramid, and the whole thing fits on a page.

Three properties that make it the right working artifact:
1. **It grows.** The Day 1 guess and the final storyline are the same document at different
   maturities. No handoff, no re-derivation.
2. **It forces precision.** You cannot hide a vague claim in an outline the way you can behind
   a chart.
3. **It exposes contradictions.** *"A storyline will clarify elements that may be ambiguous in
   the PowerPoint presentation and even bring to the surface hidden contradictions that often
   survive between, say, pages 3 and 26 of a PowerPoint pack."*

### Synthesize continuously, not at the end
> "Although synthesis appears, formally speaking, as the penultimate step in the process, it
> should happen throughout. Ideally, after you have made almost any analytical progress, you
> should attempt to articulate the 'Day 1' or 'Week 1' answer."

### Don't synthesize in PowerPoint
> "**PowerPoint is not a good tool for synthesis:** It is poor at highlighting logical
> connections."

This is the firm's own doctrine and it is the strongest available argument for a
storyline-before-slides gate.

### Keep it visible
> "Many teams find that posting the storyline or storyboard on the team-room wall helps keep
> the thinking focused. It also helps in bringing the client along."

## 4. The four distinctiveness practices

Method alone isn't enough. The paper names four habits that separate strong problem solvers:

| Practice | What it is | Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| **EXPAND** | Construct multiple perspectives — see the problem from many angles | Change the point of view (what would a customer see?); change elements of the problem (what changes if the client acquires a competitor?) |
| **LINK** | Identify relationships between issues, analyses, findings | — |
| **DISTILL** | Find the essence | — |
| **LEAD** | Stay ahead / step back | Take *time* — the paper names time as one of its most powerful tools |

## 5. Prioritizing

Dave McKinsey tip 5: **prioritize your hypotheses for impact.** The paper's process is
structure → **prioritize the issues** → plan analyses → conduct analyses → synthesize →
recommend. Prioritization sits before analysis, not after, precisely to avoid boiling the ocean.

Tip 26 has the deck-side corollary: **place low-impact resolutions in the appendix to show they
have been considered but ruled out.** Considered-and-rejected is evidence of rigor; unmentioned
is a hole a sceptic drives through.

Tip 27: **prioritize recommendations in impact order, sequential order, or emotional order** —
pick deliberately.

## 6. Ghosting the deck before you have the answer

Dave McKinsey tip 6: *"'Ghost out' your story on paper using the situation-complication-
resolution framework."* Tip 7: *"Test your hypotheses and iterate your story."*

A **ghost deck** (a.k.a. ghost pack) is the slide-shaped form of the Day 1 answer: every slide
present, every title written as the claim you *expect* to prove, and the body sketched as the
exhibit you'd need to prove it. Building it early tells you exactly which analyses matter — and
which ones you were about to run for no reason.

## 7. What this means for a deck-building toolkit

- The **dump** is the raw problem-definition material; the worksheet's six boxes are a natural
  dump-gate checklist.
- The **storyline artifact should be a dot-dash memo**, in plain text, that grows — not a
  slide deck and not a JSON blob you can't read aloud.
- **Epistemic status matters.** A hypothesis is not a finding. Anything unproven that reaches a
  title must be labelled as such, or the deck overclaims.
- **Ruled-out options belong in the appendix**, not in silence.
- The three distinctiveness questions — *is it falsifiable, is it non-obvious, does it point to
  action* — are the same three tests a good action title must pass. That is not a coincidence;
  a title is a hypothesis that survived.
